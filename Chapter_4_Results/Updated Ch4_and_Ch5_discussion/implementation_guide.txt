5.4.1  Feature-Level Evidence: The United Kingdom and Sweden

Table 5.1 moves the preceding discussion from conceptual assertion to
data-backed evidence, examining the macro-financial inputs that the E1-EBM
ensemble received for the United Kingdom and Sweden in 2004–2006, with Spain
as the reference case for a correctly identified fundamental crisis. Features
are restricted to those ranked consistently among the model's principal
predictors: credit growth, house price dynamics, term spread, and short-term
interest rate conditions.

Spain's profile is unambiguous. Demeaned credit growth of +0.078 represents
a substantial deviation above Spain's own historical norm — raw credit growth
of 19.8 per cent per annum, more than double the United Kingdom's 9.3 per cent
and Sweden's 7.4 per cent — consistent with the domestic credit-boom precursor
pattern on which the model is trained.

For the United Kingdom, the demeaned credit growth figure is −0.004:
statistically indistinguishable from zero, indicating that UK credit expansion
in 2004–2006 was at its own historical norm, not above it. The term spread
declined monotonically from +1.40 pp (2004) to −0.12 pp (2006) as the Bank
of England tightened, signalling a contracting rather than accommodative
financial environment. On the feature evidence available to it, the model's
low assigned probability (0.171) was internally consistent. The failure is one
of scope: the UK's 2007–2008 stress was concentrated in the wholesale funding
dependency of institutions such as Northern Rock — a structural vulnerability
that leaves no trace in credit growth, house price, or yield-curve features.

Sweden presents the analytically more instructive case. Demeaned credit growth
is −0.002, nearly identical to the United Kingdom, ruling out excessive credit
expansion as the driver of the model's high probability (0.783). The
differentiating signals are monetary: Sweden's short-term interest rate stood
5.60 percentage points below its own historical mean in 2004–2006, compared
with 3.57 percentage points below for the United Kingdom — materially more
accommodative conditions relative to Sweden's own long-run norm. The term
spread remained consistently positive (+1.069 pp demeaned) throughout the
period. EBM's non-linear shape functions, operating across all 41 features,
interpreted this combination of unusually accommodative rates and a steepening
yield curve as consistent with a pre-crisis financial environment. A banking
crisis did occur in Sweden in 2008 (target_h2 = 1), confirming that the macro
signals were real; the stress was transmitted via contagion from international
financial markets rather than domestic credit-boom dynamics, the mechanism the
model's feature set was built to detect.

Together, these two cases give concrete, data-backed content to the crisis
heterogeneity argument in Section 5.4. The model functions as a domestic
credit-boom and accommodative-conditions detector. Its systematic misclassification
at the boundary of that mechanism — a funding-channel crisis in the United
Kingdom, a contagion crisis in Sweden — reflects the scope of its feature design
rather than failures of discrimination per se.
