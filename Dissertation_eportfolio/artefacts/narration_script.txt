Machine Learning Early Warning Systems for Banking Crises. A dissertation by Pavlos Papachristos, for the Master of Science in Artificial Intelligence at the University of Essex Online.

This is a spoken summary of the dissertation, with particular attention to the methodology and to the results in Chapters Four, Five, and Six.


Part One. The research question, aims, and hypotheses.

Machine learning early warning systems for banking crises often look highly accurate when they are tested using an expanding window. The problem is that, in that design, the later test folds quietly absorb information from crisis periods that a real, forward-looking forecaster would never have had at the time. This dissertation asks a sharper question. Do these models still work when they are tested strictly on future crises they could not have seen? And when they fail, is the failure caused by too little data, by the choice of model, or by something deeper, namely structural instability in the underlying process itself?

The research aim is therefore twofold. First, to investigate the extent to which machine learning early warning systems achieve genuinely prospective predictive performance. And second, to characterise the structural conditions under which their generalisation fails, using an interpretable, non-linear model that makes the failure mechanism directly visible.

The study evaluates a deliberate sequence of models. Model one is logistic regression, the classical early warning baseline. Model two is a Random Forest, the black-box, non-linear benchmark. Model two point five is the Explainable Boosting Machine, referred to throughout as the E B M. This is the central instrument of the study, because its shape functions are the fitted model itself, not a post-hoc approximation, so its learned structure can be read directly. Model three is XGBoost augmented with FinBERT sentiment from central bank speeches. And Model four is an ensemble that averages the Explainable Boosting Machine with the Random Forest.

Three hypotheses were pre-specified. Hypothesis one, that the sentiment-augmented model achieves higher discrimination than the macro-only benchmark. Hypothesis two, that at least one FinBERT sentiment feature ranks in the top five by importance. And hypothesis three, that there is a measurable trade-off between accuracy and interpretability across the model sequence. The Explainable Boosting Machine then extends hypothesis three, by testing whether interpretability and accuracy can, in fact, be achieved together within the non-linear class.


Part Two. Methodology.

The design is a quantitative, empirical study using secondary panel data, built as a two-stage pipeline. Stage one evaluates the macro-only models on a long panel running from 1989 to 2020, which spans several pre-crisis episodes. Stage two evaluates the sentiment-augmented models on the shorter overlap window, from 2003 to 2020, where the central bank speech data is available.

The macroeconomic data comes from the Jorda, Schularick and Taylor Macrohistory Database, covering eighteen advanced economies. After constructing the lagged features, the usable Stage one panel contains 569 country-year observations. Of these, 37 are positive, meaning a systemic banking crisis begins within the following two years. That is a base rate of just 6.5 per cent. This is a small, highly imbalanced dataset, and that constraint shapes the interpretation of everything that follows.

There is a still more important point about these positives. The 37 positive labels correspond to only 19 distinct crisis onsets, and those onsets cluster into just three systemic episodes: the early 1990s European and Nordic cluster, Japan in the late 1990s, and the 2007 to 2008 Global Financial Crisis. The financial crisis alone accounts for about sixty-five per cent of all the positives. So the effective number of independent crisis regimes available for learning is three, not thirty-seven. This is the single most important limitation in the study.

The sentiment data comes from the Bank for International Settlements speech archive: over sixteen thousand central bank speeches, processed through FinBERT, a financial language model, to produce sentiment scores aggregated to the country-year level. A candid limitation is recorded here. FinBERT is an English-language model, and the share of non-English or machine-translated text feeding it cannot be reconstructed, so this is disclosed as an unresolved limitation rather than a quantified one.

The prediction target is a two-year-ahead crisis indicator. There are forty-one macroeconomic features, built from credit, money, interest rates, house prices, government debt, inflation, the current account, real output, and the term spread, all expressed at lags of one to three years and demeaned using expanding, country-specific means to prevent any forward leakage. For the sentiment model, twenty FinBERT-derived features are added, giving a combined set of sixty-one features.

An important point on procedure. All model hyperparameters were fixed in advance. The training notebooks perform no grid search and no nested cross-validation search, so the reported metrics carry no selection bias from tuning. Class imbalance is handled by weighting, not by resampling. Random seeds are fixed at forty-two throughout, and the full code is publicly available.

The evaluation framework is where the study is most demanding. There are three layers. First, expanding-window cross-validation, which is the conventional approach, using twenty-seven folds in Stage one. Second, and most importantly, strict temporal validation, which is the primary prospective test: the model is trained only on data before a crisis and tested on a later crisis it has never seen. The critical case here is Split B, which trains on everything from 1989 to 2002 and tests on 2003 to 2011, that is, it must forecast the Global Financial Crisis using only pre-crisis history. Third, leave-one-country-out validation, holding out each country in turn.

The key benchmark is a ROC area under the curve of zero point seven five, taken from the crisis prediction literature. The primary metrics are the ROC area for discrimination, the precision-recall area, which is more informative under class imbalance, the Brier Score for probability accuracy, and the expected calibration error.


Part Three. Chapter Four. The results.

We begin with the primary model comparison. Under the Stage one expanding-window design, on the long macro panel, the models line up as follows. Logistic regression, Model one, achieves a ROC area of about zero point six one. The bridge logistic specification reaches about zero point six five. The Random Forest, Model two, achieves about zero point six four. And the Explainable Boosting Machine, Model two point five, achieves zero point seven five three, the strongest score, and the only one to reach the literature benchmark, using exactly the same features and the same cross-validation design as the Random Forest.

A paired statistical test, the DeLong test, confirms that the roughly zero point one two ROC gap between the Explainable Boosting Machine and the Random Forest is statistically significant. However, and this qualification runs through the whole chapter, this describes point estimates within the same regime-specific window. It is not evidence of prospective validity, because ninety-three per cent of the scored positives come from the single financial crisis episode. It is therefore reported as a within-regime finding, not as a general property of interpretable models.

Now to Stage two, which must be understood correctly. Because the crisis events concentrate in 2007, the precision-recall metric is computable in only one fold. So Stage two is not a temporal forecast. It is a cross-sectional case study of the 2007 crisis, measuring how well each model ranks the eighteen countries by crisis risk within that single episode. On this 2007 cross-section, all the models score highly: the Random Forest reaches a ROC area of about zero point nine four, the Explainable Boosting Machine about zero point nine six, and the sentiment model, Model three, about zero point nine three. These high numbers reflect the concentration of the crisis in one year, not genuine forecasting skill.

This brings us to hypothesis one, on sentiment. The sentiment model does show a directional precision-recall advantage over the macro-only Random Forest, but the confidence interval for that difference spans zero, so it cannot be distinguished from noise. More tellingly, the comparison confounds two changes at once: it changes both the algorithm and the feature set. So the study performs a careful decomposition, varying only one thing at a time. Holding the feature set fixed and changing only the algorithm, from XGBoost to the Explainable Boosting Machine, the precision-recall score rises by about zero point zero four in the boosting machine's favour. But holding the algorithm fixed at the Explainable Boosting Machine and adding sentiment to the macro features, the precision-recall score falls by about zero point zero eight. In other words, once the algorithm is held constant, sentiment actually reduces discrimination, and the bootstrap confidence interval for that effect spans zero by a wide margin. The verdict on hypothesis one is: not confirmed. The apparent sentiment gain is better explained by the change in algorithm than by any sentiment signal.

Now to the most important result in the dissertation: the strict temporal validation, in Section four point five. This is the true test of forward-looking performance. In Split B, forecasting the Global Financial Crisis from pre-crisis data only, the Random Forest achieves a ROC area of just zero point four eight one, and the Explainable Boosting Machine achieves zero point three three three. Both are below the zero point seven five benchmark. Both are, in fact, at or below the level of a random classifier. Let that land. The best in-sample model, the Explainable Boosting Machine, collapses the furthest when it must genuinely predict a future crisis. Neither extending the training history by fifteen years, nor changing the algorithm, produces a model that generalises prospectively to the crisis. This is the empirical anchor of the whole study: every expanding-window number reported elsewhere is an upper bound on real prospective performance.

The gap between the two models here, with the more flexible Explainable Boosting Machine collapsing further than the Random Forest, is directionally consistent with the idea that greater flexibility produces more severe regime-specific overfitting. But it rests on only six pre-crisis training positives, so it is read as directional, not confirmed. The stronger evidence for that mechanism comes from the shape functions, which we reach shortly.

The leave-one-country-out validation, in Section four point five point two, holds out each of fifteen countries in turn. The Explainable Boosting Machine records a higher mean ROC area, about zero point eight nine, against the Random Forest's zero point eight four, with lower variability, and the ordering holds under the more robust median and multi-event statistics. Japan is a notable exception, where both models perform close to random, consistent with asset-deflation crisis dynamics being poorly represented in the training distribution.

Next, feature attribution, in Section four point six. Using SHAP attribution on the sentiment model, the macroeconomic features account for about seventy-four per cent of the total signal, and sentiment for about twenty-six per cent. The single most important feature, by a substantial margin, is the term spread at a three-year lag. Crucially, no sentiment feature ranks in the top five. So hypothesis two, which required a sentiment feature in the top five, is not supported in its strict formulation. And both SHAP attribution and the Explainable Boosting Machine's own native importance independently identify the same top feature, the three-year-lagged term spread, a triangulation showing the finding is not an artefact of one particular method.

We now reach the centrepiece of the dissertation, in Section four point seven: the Explainable Boosting Machine interpretability benchmark, and specifically its shape function analysis. The logic is this. Because the boosting machine's shape functions are the fitted model itself, and not a post-hoc approximation, any instability we see in them cannot be blamed on approximation error. That confound is eliminated by construction. What remains is a genuine property of what the model has learned.

The test fits the model separately on two nested training windows: an early, pre-crisis run-up window, from 2003 to 2006, and a crisis-inclusive window, from 2003 to 2010. It then compares the shape functions. The finding is striking. For the term spread at a three-year lag, the model's single most important feature, the two curves are not merely scaled versions of one another. They are qualitatively different, and they cross. This means that the same value of the term spread is associated with an increase in crisis risk under one training window and a decrease under the other. The learned relationship between the feature and the risk actually reverses sign, depending on which period the model was trained on. The instability grows across the lags, and it is largest precisely at the most important feature. This makes one mechanism behind the prospective collapse directly legible, rather than merely inferred: a mapping learned before the crisis, applied to a structurally different crisis environment, produces exactly the kind of errors we see in the below-random forecast.

Two explanations are considered for this, and the study is honest that the data cannot fully separate them. The first is genuine non-stationarity in the data-generating process, meaning the real relationship between the economy and crisis risk changes between regimes. The second is regime-specific model variance, meaning the thin, correlated sample in the early window simply produces noisy estimates. The study weighs both, and finds independent support for the first from the structural break test, which we come to shortly.

A robustness check, in Section four point seven point four, confirms that these results are not an artefact of the chosen settings: across five alternative hyperparameter configurations, the ROC area varies by only about zero point zero zero seven, and every alternative falls inside the baseline's confidence interval. The baseline was not cherry-picked.

On calibration, in Section four point eight, the Explainable Boosting Machine achieves the lowest expected calibration error, about zero point zero eight, and it does so without any post-hoc calibration step, unlike the sentiment model. Its native probability calibration is good in its own right.

The auxiliary statistical tests, in Section four point nine, are important because they discipline the machine learning results. Across a family of eighty-four Granger causality tests, on credit growth and the term spread, exactly one survives rigorous multiple-testing correction: United States credit growth at a three-year lag, with a p-value of zero point zero zero zero zero one five. This clears the strict Bonferroni threshold by a factor of about forty, and is the single most robust association in the whole battery. Notably, none of the term spread results survive correction, despite the term spread's prominence in the machine learning attribution, so that prominence is treated as a within-episode correlation, not a confirmed causal signal.

For sentiment, the result is a clean null. Across a family of one hundred and fifty-six Granger tests, not one survives correction. This definitively reinforces the finding that sentiment carries no robust, generalisable signal.

The structural break test, a Chow-type likelihood ratio test at the 2007 boundary, provides the independent evidence mentioned earlier. For credit growth, the test statistic is fourteen point two four two, with a p-value of zero point zero zero six six: a statistically significant structural break. The lag-one credit growth coefficient actually reverses sign and grows substantially across the break. For the term spread, by contrast, the test statistic is four point two one, with a p-value of zero point three eight: the break is not statistically significant, most likely because there are too few crisis events to give the test power. So the credit-growth break is presented as reasonably firm evidence of non-stationarity; the term-spread instability is directionally consistent but carries less weight.

Then, in Section four point ten, the ensemble extension. The equal-weighted average of the Explainable Boosting Machine and the Random Forest achieves the lowest Brier Score of any configuration in the case study, about zero point zero five six, at a high ROC area of about zero point nine six. The disagreement analysis is instructive: the two models disagree about two point eight times more at crisis observations than at calm ones, which is exactly where averaging is most valuable. However, the ensemble's precision-recall score is lower than the Explainable Boosting Machine alone, because averaging dilutes the boosting machine's sharper probabilities. So the ensemble suits calibration and overall probability accuracy, while the single boosting machine suits within-episode ranking.

An exploratory, post-hoc cross-sectional analysis of the 2007 episode finds that the ensemble assigns systematically higher probabilities to fundamental, credit-boom crises, a mean of about zero point seven, than to contagion-driven countries or to non-crisis countries, at about zero point three to zero point four. This ordering is coherent with the model's design, since its macro features are built to detect domestic credit-cycle imbalances, not externally transmitted stress.

To summarise the hypothesis assessment, in Section four point eleven. Hypothesis one, on sentiment discrimination, is not confirmed: it is directional only, and the algorithm change explains at least as much as sentiment. Hypothesis two, on sentiment feature importance, is not supported in its strict form. And hypothesis three, on the accuracy-interpretability trade-off, is partially supported, but not in the simple monotonic form originally expected: interpretability and strong in-sample accuracy can coexist, but interpretability confers no prospective robustness, and may even increase vulnerability under regime change.


Part Four. Chapter Five. Discussion.

The discussion draws out four principal findings. First, extending the macro training history does not resolve the generalisation problem: even with fifteen extra years of crisis history, the model remains at or below random in the only informative strict test. Second, sentiment augmentation shows no reliable signal, once the algorithm effect is properly separated and multiple-testing correction is applied. Third, the Explainable Boosting Machine interpretability benchmark, the most theoretically significant contribution, shows that native transparency is diagnostically valuable but does not by itself buy robustness. And fourth, synthesising the rest, the combined evidence is most consistent with structural instability in the mapping between macroeconomic indicators and crisis risk across historical periods, rather than with insufficient data or a simple model-selection problem, although estimation instability from sparse samples cannot be excluded.

The discussion then deepens the interpretability finding with a further, telling piece of evidence. When the Explainable Boosting Machine is refitted on the pre-crisis sample alone, the entire feature hierarchy changes. The three lags of the term spread, which dominate in the pooled model, all but vanish, and the leading predictors instead become inflation and the government debt-to-output ratio. This is economically coherent: the early 1990s Nordic crises and Japan followed episodes of high inflation and fiscal deterioration, whereas the financial crisis was preceded by term-spread compression under low inflation. So the pooled model's apparent reliance on the term spread is largely an inheritance from the crisis observations that supply most of its positive cases. This is not just a change in the shape of a relationship; it is a change in which features the model relies on at all, and it is precisely the failure mode that the extra data and flexibility tested here did not overcome.

On crisis heterogeneity, in Section five point four, the discussion makes an important structural point. Crisis chronologies record when a crisis happened, but not the mechanism. At least four mechanisms matter: fundamental crises driven by domestic credit booms; contagion crises transmitted through financial linkages without domestic imbalance; speculative attacks on fixed exchange rates; and exogenous shocks such as the pandemic. Training on a single, undifferentiated label implicitly, and wrongly, assumes the same features are relevant to all crisis types.

This is illustrated with a three-country case study: Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. Spain is a clean hit: its credit growth, at nearly twenty per cent a year, and its house-price growth fit the classic domestic-boom pattern, and the model ranks it high-risk. Sweden is a true positive, but for the wrong reasons: its credit growth was only moderate, and the model's high probability actually rests on shared monetary and yield-curve conditions, not on a mechanism-specific signal. The United Kingdom is the instructive failure. The model assigns it a low probability, because United Kingdom stress in 2007 and 2008 originated on the liability side, in short-term wholesale funding of mortgage books, rather than in the asset-side credit imbalances the model's features are built to detect. This is a scope condition, not an unexplained error: the model fails precisely where the crisis mechanism lies outside its feature set. And this legibility, the ability to see exactly why the model errs, is the interpretability contribution.

The discussion then sets out five implications for early warning system design. First, extending training history is necessary but not sufficient; an operational system needs a regime-classification layer or a portfolio of mechanism-specific models, not a single general-purpose classifier. Second, interpretable non-linear models should be used as primary diagnostic benchmarks, for their ability to reveal whether learned relationships are regime-stable, but not presumed to be prospectively superior. Third, ensemble averaging is most defensible as a probability-smoothing device for calibration, not for discrimination. Fourth, central bank sentiment does not contribute a robust signal and should be treated as hypothesis-generating. And fifth, United States credit growth at a three-year lag is the only auxiliary predictor surviving rigorous correction, consistent with the credit-boom literature.

Four practical recommendations follow. One: strict, prospective validation across crisis regimes must be mandatory, not optional, and models that fail it must not be sold as predictively useful without explicit disclosure. Two: interpretable models should be used as diagnostic benchmarks, with shape-function stability treated as a first-order supervisory criterion. Three: ensemble averaging should be considered as an output-smoothing mechanism, accepting the trade-off between calibration and within-episode ranking. And four: sentiment augmentation requires validation across multiple, structurally distinct crisis episodes before any operational use.

The limitations are stated frankly. The dominant constraint is that the case-study evaluation rests on crisis events in a single year, 2007, so the strong cross-sectional numbers must be read as directional and regime-specific. The below-random strict temporal result remains the binding evidence on genuine forward-looking deployment. A direct replication on a second multi-country episode is simply not available within this panel, so the conclusions are hypothesis-generating about behaviour under one structural regime, not confirmed findings about early warning systems in general. The model class is also bounded: recurrent architectures such as long short-term memory networks were not tested. But the study notes that a sequence model learns dependency structure within a mapping; it cannot repair a mapping whose sign reverses, which is what the structural break evidence indicates.


Part Five. Chapter Six. Conclusions.

The dissertation reaches five conclusions. The first, and most important, is that the prospective failure of macro-based early warning systems is not an artefact of insufficient training data. With the full pre-crisis history available, the model still achieves a ROC area of only zero point four eight one in strict temporal validation, below random. The gap between expanding-window and strict temporal performance shows precisely why the training-volume explanation fails: the expanding window inflates performance by quietly absorbing later crisis regimes into the training data, and once that inflation is removed, the additional years contribute no genuine prospective benefit.

The second conclusion is that the interpretability-accuracy trade-off is not binary. The Explainable Boosting Machine matches or exceeds its black-box counterparts in-sample while remaining natively interpretable. But under strict validation both fail, so neither algorithm generalises prospectively regardless of interpretability.

The third conclusion is the dissertation's most theoretically significant contribution: the Explainable Boosting Machine's shape functions make training-window instability directly visible. Because the shape functions are the model itself, the observed instability, including the sign reversal at the most important feature, cannot be an artefact of attribution. Supported by the significant structural break in credit growth, this is the most direct evidence that static, pooled early warning designs face a structural limitation in a non-stationary environment, shown rather than merely inferred.

The fourth conclusion is that sentiment augmentation contributes a directional but unstable signal that does not survive algorithm control or auxiliary statistical testing, and macro features carry the dominant measured signal. And the fifth, a supplementary result, is that ensemble averaging lowers overall probability error within the 2007 cross-section, achieving the lowest Brier Score of any configuration.

The final reflection captures the arc of the work. The dissertation began with a narrow model-comparison question: does sentiment improve crisis prediction? It ended with a broader, theoretical contribution about the limits of predictive modelling under distribution shift. The central lesson is about the relationship between machine learning and non-stationarity. A model trained on historical data represents that history; when the process that generated it changes, that representation becomes a misspecification. The contribution of the Explainable Boosting Machine is to make this visible rather than inferred. That visibility does not solve the non-stationarity problem, but it is a precondition for addressing it, because a practitioner who can see where a model's learned mapping breaks down can apply the appropriate scepticism, communicate the limitation to policy audiences, and design better, regime-aware systems. The value of interpretable machine learning here is not superior accuracy, but superior legibility: knowing not only what a model predicts, but the conditions under which that prediction should not be trusted.

This concludes the spoken summary of the dissertation.
